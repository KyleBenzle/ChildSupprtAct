# Families First Act of 2027

> **Discussion draft — not an official congressional document.** This proposal has not been introduced, sponsored, scored by the Congressional Budget Office (CBO), or reviewed by House or Senate Legislative Counsel.

The Families First Act of 2027 would change how the federal government divides a fixed pool of child-support performance incentive payments among state and territorial Title IV-D programs. Its central policy choice is to reduce the formula's dependence on dollars collected and amounts owed, while continuing to reward paternity establishment, support-order establishment, current payments, and payments on arrears.

The project starts from a concern: government should not have a financial stake in unnecessarily adversarial family outcomes. The evidence supports the narrower statement that the current formula rewards collections and that some Title IV-D money supports state and local agencies involved in support establishment and enforcement. It does **not** establish that federal law pays states for sole custody, denying parenting time, or keeping a child below a particular overnight threshold. No causal study found for this project shows that the federal incentive formula causes judges to maximize parent-child separation.

That distinction matters. Durable reform should be based on what the statutes and data actually show.

## Executive summary

- Title IV-D of the Social Security Act is a federal-state child-support program created in 1975. It locates parents, establishes parentage and support orders, and collects and distributes support.
- The largest federal funding stream generally reimburses **66% of allowable state administrative costs**. A separate, much smaller performance-incentive pool is divided among jurisdictions.
- Under [42 U.S.C. § 658a](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/t42/s658a/), the current incentive formula measures paternity establishment, cases with support orders, current-support collections, arrears cases paying, and cost-effectiveness. The calculation uses a “State collections base”; certain current and former public-assistance collections receive double weight.
- **Custody type, parenting-time percentage, and number of overnights are not federal incentive measures.** Federal IV-D services generally do not decide custody or visitation.
- A support order can exist in shared-parenting and equal-time cases. Equal time does not necessarily mean zero support because state formulas may account for income differences, health insurance, childcare, and other costs.
- The draft replaces the collections base with an order-adjusted base and removes cost-effectiveness from the calculation. It does not amend state custody law, mandate 50/50 parenting, or directly fund mediation.
- The proposal is budget-neutral only with respect to the fixed incentive pool. It would redistribute that pool among states, create reporting and systems costs, and could change state behavior.

## What current federal law actually rewards

### Two funding streams that should not be confused

1. **Federal financial participation (FFP).** Under [42 U.S.C. § 655](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/t42/s655/), the federal government generally reimburses 66% of allowable Title IV-D administrative expenditures. This is not a payment per separated family and is not conditioned on sole custody.
2. **Performance incentives.** Under [42 U.S.C. § 658a](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/t42/s658a/), a capped national pool is divided according to relative state performance. Incentive funds must be reinvested in the child-support program and ordinarily cannot be used as the state share to draw additional federal match.

For FY2023, HHS reported approximately **$29.6 billion collected**, **12.7 million children served**, and **$4.37 collected for every program dollar spent**. Ninety-seven percent of collections went to families and 3% reimbursed public-assistance dollars. See the [HHS FY2023 national infographic](https://www.acf.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/documents/ocse/2023_infographic_national.pdf).

### The five current incentive measures

| Measure | What it asks | What it does not ask |
|---|---|---|
| Paternity establishment | What percentage of the relevant children have parentage established? | Who has custody? |
| Support-order establishment | What percentage of IV-D cases have a support order? | Whether parenting is sole, shared, or equal-time |
| Current-payment performance | What percentage of current support due was collected? | How many overnights each parent has |
| Arrears performance | What percentage of arrears cases received a payment? | Whether conflict was resolved |
| Cost-effectiveness | How many dollars were collected per program dollar spent? | Child well-being or family relationships |

The state collections base generally consists of collections, with double weighting for specified current and former public-assistance collections. A state's score determines its **share of a fixed national pool**; it does not receive one federal dollar for every support dollar ordered or collected.

The pool was **$678.8 million in actual FY2023 payments**. HHS announced pools of **$735.5 million for FY2025** and **$755.4 million for FY2026**. By comparison, preliminary FY2025 federal reimbursement of state administrative costs was about **$4.22 billion**. Sources and qualifications appear in the [research notes](Legislative_Package/05_Sources_and_Research_Notes.pdf).

### What can fairly be called an incentive

The collections base creates a direct mathematical link: other things equal, collecting more support increases a state's incentive base. Establishing more orders and improving payment rates also improve performance scores. State and local child-support agencies, court clerks, attorneys, sheriffs, and other entities may perform eligible IV-D work under cooperative arrangements. GAO documented such arrangements and the 66% federal match in [GAO-06-491](https://www.gao.gov/assets/a250747.html).

But the next causal steps are unproven. The project found no evidence that:

- a judge receives more money for awarding sole rather than shared custody;
- a state must give one parent “100% custody” to maximize federal funds;
- a state loses all federal funding when a parent has 30, 90, or 182 overnights;
- Title IV-D incentive dollars are unrestricted operating revenue for an entire family court; or
- the current formula has been shown to cause custody decisions.

The accurate critique is: **the federal formula rewards collection-centered outputs while omitting parenting-time, conflict-resolution, and child-well-being outcomes.** Whether that structure changes custody outcomes remains an empirical question.

## History

- **1950–1974:** Congress gradually added tools to recover support in public-assistance cases.
- **1975:** The Social Services Amendments of 1974, effective in 1975, created Title IV-D as a federal-state program. Its original design was closely connected to recovering welfare costs, although services for families not receiving cash assistance later became permanent.
- **1984:** The Child Support Enforcement Amendments strengthened enforcement and used collection-based incentives.
- **1996:** The Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act expanded automated enforcement, the federal parent locator system, new-hire reporting, and related tools. This Clinton-era law did **not** create the current five-measure formula.
- **1998:** The [Child Support Performance and Incentive Act, Pub. L. 105-200](https://www.congress.gov/105/plaws/publ200/PLAW-105publ200.pdf), also signed during the Clinton administration, created the current performance-based structure, phased in beginning in FY2000. It replaced the prior incentive system rather than originating federal child-support enforcement.
- **2005–2007:** The Deficit Reduction Act ended the practice of using incentives as the state share for additional federal match, effective FY2008; temporary relief applied in FY2009–FY2010.

For a detailed chronology, see CRS, [*The Child Support Enforcement Program: A Legislative History*](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R44423/R44423.4.pdf).

## What the draft changes

The bill amends Social Security Act § 458 (42 U.S.C. § 658a).

### Proposed order-adjusted base

```text
order-adjusted base = max(0, benchmark × case-months − current support owed)
```

A “case-month” is a month in which current support is owed. At the draft's bracketed **$1,000 monthly benchmark**, a case-month with $0 owed contributes 1,000 units of weight, $250 contributes 750, $500 contributes 500, and $1,000 or more contributes zero. These units divide the existing fixed pool; they are **not dollar-for-dollar federal payments**.

The draft also removes cost-effectiveness, retains measures for parentage, orders, current payments and arrears payments, requires reliable data, and requires HHS to publish the inputs and results.

### What the draft does not do

It does not:

- create a federal 50/50 custody presumption;
- direct judges how to allocate parenting time;
- make custody or mediation outcomes a performance metric;
- change state child-support guidelines;
- guarantee lower support in shared-parenting cases;
- prevent support orders where one parent has no overnights; or
- appropriate a new family-conflict-resolution grant.

If the intended goal is to pay states for successfully resolving family conflict, the draft needs a second design stage: define measurable outcomes such as durable stipulated parenting plans, safe mediation completion, reduced relitigation, parenting-time compliance, time to disposition, and safety outcomes. Every metric would need auditing and safeguards against pressuring survivors or parents into unsafe agreements.

## Ohio: custody, parenting time, and support

Ohio is not accurately described as a universal “100% mother-custody state.” Its rules depend on marital status and court orders.

### Married parents and court proceedings

[Ohio Rev. Code § 3109.04](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-3109.04) directs courts to allocate parental rights under the child's best interests. Shared parenting is authorized. If no qualifying shared-parenting plan is before the court or no plan is found to be in the child's best interest, the court designates one parent the residential parent and legal custodian and allocates parenting time and other rights. The statute is not a presumption that mothers win.

[Ohio Rev. Code § 3109.051](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-3109.051) generally calls for a just and reasonable parenting-time order and, where possible and consistent with the child's interests, frequent and continuing contact.

### Unmarried parents

[Ohio Rev. Code § 3109.042](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-3109.042) makes an unmarried mother the sole residential parent and legal custodian **until a court issues an order**. When a court makes that designation, it must treat mother and father as standing on equal footing. Parentage acknowledgment alone should not be confused with a custody order.

Similar initial-custody rules for unmarried parents exist elsewhere, but they differ materially. Examples include [Michigan](https://legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=MCL-722-1006), [Florida](https://leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&Search_String=744.3145&URL=0700-0799%2F0744%2FSections%2F0744.301.html%2F%2F%2F), and [Georgia](https://www.legis.ga.gov/api/document/docs/default-source/joint-features-document-library/t18-t19-%28v16%29-pdf.pdf). These are defaults before an agreement or adjudication, not proof that courts always award mothers sole custody. A 50-state label such as “mother-custody state” obscures differences among parentage, legitimation, initial custody, legal custody, physical custody, and parenting time.

### The Ohio overnight rule

There is no federal “less than 30 overnights” or “zero overnights” rule that guarantees maximum child support.

Ohio's relevant bright line is [Ohio Rev. Code § 3119.051](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-3119.051): when a court-ordered schedule equals or exceeds **90 overnights per year**, the annual individual support obligation ordinarily receives a **10% reduction**, subject to statutory exceptions. Under [Ohio Rev. Code § 3119.23](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-3119.23), extended parenting time and extraordinary parenting-time costs may also support a deviation.

Therefore:

- 0 versus 30 overnights does not itself determine whether an order is “100%”;
- fewer than 90 overnights does not eliminate authority to consider applicable deviations;
- 90 overnights triggers a 10% adjustment, not elimination of support;
- 182/183 overnights does not automatically produce a $0 order; and
- federal incentive law does not change at any of those thresholds.

## National custody data: what can and cannot be inferred

The Census Bureau estimated that in 2022:

- **13.9 million** parents lived with **22.2 million** children under 21 while the other parent lived elsewhere;
- these children were **27.1%** of children under 21 living in families;
- **78.2%** of those residential (“custodial” in the survey) parents were mothers and **21.8%** were fathers; and
- only **37.3%** of custodial parents had a legal child-support agreement, down from 55.1% in 2003.

See Census, [*Custodial Parents and Their Child Support: 2022*](https://www2.census.gov/library/publications/2025/demo/p60-285.pdf) and the [detailed tables](https://www.census.gov/data/tables/2022/demo/families/2022-cps-childsupport.html).

These figures do **not** prove what courts ordered. Census uses household residence, not legal custody, and its data include informal arrangements and agreed outcomes. Observed differences may reflect caregiving patterns, agreements, non-litigation, unmarried-parent defaults, economics, judicial decisions, or selection into the survey category.

State law changes frequently. As of this research, sources identify Arkansas, Florida, Kentucky, Missouri, and West Virginia as states with some form of rebuttable presumption of equal or approximately equal parenting time. Exact scope and safety exceptions differ, so the controlling statute must be checked before making a 50-state claim. The [ABA's 2025 custody-statute chart](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/family_law/resources/family-law-50-states/2025-child-custody/) is a starting point, not a substitute for current law.

## Evidence about shared physical custody

Research often associates shared physical custody—commonly defined in studies as roughly 30–70% time with each parent—with better average child outcomes than lone physical custody. A 2023 systematic review reports that pattern across much of the literature while emphasizing competing explanations, family selection, differing definitions, and limits on causal inference. See Steinbach and Augustijn, [PLOS ONE systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10313020/).

That evidence does not justify an irrebuttable rule. Families choosing or receiving shared arrangements can differ in income, conflict, proximity, parental capacity, and prior involvement. Safety screening is essential. A systematic review of 51 studies associated interparental coercive control with multiple adverse child and family outcomes. See [*Interparental Coercive Control and Child and Family Outcomes*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/15248380221139243).

A defensible policy can favor meaningful relationships with both fit parents while preserving individualized findings and strong exceptions for domestic violence, coercive control, abuse, neglect, serious impairment, impractical distance, and other risks to the child.

## Arguments for the draft

1. **Better alignment.** Raw dollars collected are not the same as child well-being or family stability.
2. **Removes a financial stake in higher obligations.** The order-adjusted base reverses the direction of the current collection-weighted base.
3. **Less emphasis on public-assistance recovery.** It eliminates double weighting of specified assistance collections.
4. **Budget discipline.** It retains the fixed, CPI-indexed pool rather than creating an uncapped entitlement.
5. **Transparency.** Required publication would expose inputs, scores, and payments.
6. **Preserves core goals.** Parentage, order establishment, current payment, and arrears payment measures remain.
7. **Opens a broader debate.** It asks whether federal funding should measure durable, safe family outcomes rather than collections alone.

## Arguments against the draft

1. **It may reward lower orders rather than resolved conflict.** The formula directly rewards lower amounts owed; it does not observe custody quality, agreement, safety, or child outcomes.
2. **Children may receive less support.** Downward pressure on guidelines or deviations could reduce household resources.
3. **No demonstrated custody causation.** The premise that current incentives alter judicial custody decisions is not empirically established.
4. **It may penalize higher-income and high-cost states.** A uniform benchmark disadvantages jurisdictions with higher wages, costs, or larger families.
5. **It creates gaming risks.** States might favor low or $0 orders, alter case practices, or misreport case-months.
6. **The metric is remote from courts.** A small statewide signal may not change individual judicial behavior.
7. **It drops cost-effectiveness.** Removing that measure may reduce pressure for efficient administration.
8. **It has administrative costs.** HHS does not collect the required case-month measure; forms, regulations, systems, and audits must change.
9. **Safety and equity are not measured.** Conflict-resolution rewards can pressure unsafe settlements unless violence, coercion, bargaining power, disability, language access, and representation are addressed.
10. **Indirect costs are unknown.** Lower collections can reduce public-assistance recovery and affect programs that count support as income.

## Quantitative risks and design choices

At the bracketed $1,000 benchmark, the FY2023 simulation redistributes the pool substantially: 27 of 54 jurisdictions gain and 27 lose. New Jersey loses about 63% relative to the reproduced current formula; several lower-order jurisdictions gain sharply. These are illustrative results, not CBO estimates.

A literal dollar-for-dollar version of “$1,000 minus the monthly order” would cost an estimated **$60.5 billion per year**, roughly 89 times the FY2023 pool. Keeping the fixed pool avoids that cost but makes the formula a competition among states: one state's larger share is another state's smaller share.

Before introduction, policymakers should consider whether to:

- adjust the benchmark for state income or living costs;
- cap annual gains and losses or phase in the change;
- weight by the number of children in a case;
- exclude $0 orders from positive weight;
- retain a revised efficiency measure;
- measure safe, durable agreements and reduced relitigation directly;
- add an independent domestic-violence and child-safety review;
- require distributional reporting by income, race, sex, disability, and assistance status; and
- pilot and evaluate the approach before nationwide implementation.

## Data limitations and unanswered questions

- HHS does not publish actual case-months with current support owed. The simulation uses 12 times year-end cases with orders minus arrears-only cases, which can overcount partial-year cases and include $0 orders.
- Published data do not reproduce audited incentive payments perfectly. The Ohio reproduction is close; some states differ materially.
- Recent HHS datasets are preliminary.
- Interstate cases, multi-child cases, voluntary payments without orders, and case closure need regulatory treatment.
- No CBO score exists.
- No causal evidence was identified linking the current formula to custody or parenting-time decisions.
- No reliable national database was identified that reports court-ordered overnight distributions for state comparison.
- “Legal custody,” “physical custody,” “residential parent,” “shared parenting,” and survey “custodial parent” are not interchangeable.

## Recommended next research

1. Obtain audited state incentive workpapers and actual case-month/order distributions from HHS.
2. Map state and county cooperative agreements to identify exactly which court functions receive IV-D reimbursement.
3. Commission a causal study comparing custody, support, and court-funding changes across states and time.
4. Build a verified 50-state table of unmarried-parent defaults, equal-parenting presumptions, overnight adjustments, and safety exceptions.
5. Ask GAO to evaluate whether federal formulas influence orders, case practices, court operations, or parenting-time litigation.
6. Ask CBO to score systems costs, public-assistance recoveries, and behavioral responses.
7. Pilot outcome measures with survivor advocates, parents, judges, child-development researchers, and administrators before attaching money to them.
